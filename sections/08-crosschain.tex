\section{Cross-Chain Exposure}
\label{sec:crosschain}

Cross-chain operation is the default; same-chain loans are a special case. Two concerns arise at different layers.

\textbf{Authorization} (protocol-level): proving that collateral was pledged on the collateral chain so the principal chain's contracts can act on it. \daloc{} uses zero-knowledge storage proofs: cryptographic attestations that a particular storage slot held a particular value at a particular block, verified against the chain's state root. A state root relay or light client makes the source chain's finalized state available on the destination chain.

\textbf{Token movement} (proof-specified): bridge hooks in the creation proof move the borrowed asset to its destination. The operator selects the admissible bridge path when assembling the proof; keepers submit the resulting transaction on-chain for execution.

\subsection{Authorization Flow}

Consider a borrower who pledges WETH on Ethereum and requests USDC on Arbitrum. The collateral-chain issuer records the pledge; a settlement program reads the CDP's storage slot from Ethereum's state root via a storage proof (a Merkle inclusion proof against the state trie, attested by a finalized state root relayed to the destination chain), producing a verifiable claim that the collateral is locked. On Arbitrum, the debt issuer validates this claim through the facts registry and issues the debt token.

Coordination relies on shared market identifiers and deterministic addressing, not real-time cross-chain messaging. Bridge latency affects capital delivery but not settlement correctness; a delayed bridge stalls execution, never produces an invalid state. The relay's trust model is load-bearing for every cross-chain position: a compromised relay could feed false finality, enabling fabricated storage proofs. Participants inherit the relay's security model alongside the oracle's.

\subsection{Deterministic Addressing}

Identical hub addresses on every supported chain, combined with deterministic \texttt{CREATE2} loan contracts, allow collateral on chain~A and debt on chain~B to reference the same loan identifier. Adding a new destination chain requires only a bridge adapter whitelisted on the hub.

\subsection{Hyperliquid Margin Agent}

For Prime Trade, the margin pipeline (Section~\ref{sec:lifecycle}) routes borrowed USDC to a per-loan \emph{margin agent} on HyperEVM~\cite{hyperliquid} at a \texttt{CREATE2} address computed at quote time. Capital enters HyperCore as margin, never the borrower's wallet. Permitted actions are restricted to perpetual trading; withdrawals, spot transfers, and external lending are blocked.

On close or liquidation, the margin agent recalls capital from HyperCore and bridges it back to Ethereum. Collateral never crosses to HyperEVM; only USDC round-trips. Figure~\ref{fig:primetrade} illustrates the full origination, active trading, and close cycle.

\subsection{Effective Leverage}

For Prime Trade, the borrowing LTV combined with venue leverage creates a multiplicative effect. For collateral worth~$C$ deposited at LTV~$\lambda$ with venue leverage~$\ell$, the notional controlled is
\begin{equation}
  N = C \cdot \lambda \cdot \ell,
  \label{eq:efflev}
\end{equation}
and the effective leverage relative to the collateral is $\lambda_{\mathrm{eff}} = N / C = \lambda \cdot \ell$.

At $\lambda = 62.5\%$ LTV with $\ell = 5\times$ venue leverage, $\lambda_{\mathrm{eff}} = \lambda \cdot \ell \approx 3.1\times$; this yields substantial notional controlled without selling the underlying. Collateral remains on Ethereum throughout.
