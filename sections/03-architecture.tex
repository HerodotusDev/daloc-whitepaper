\section{Protocol Architecture}
\label{sec:architecture}

\subsection{Order Book}

All loan activity flows through an order book maintained off-chain by each market's operator (the party that performs matching and produces zero-knowledge proofs; independent keepers submit them on-chain for execution~\cite{daloc-decent2026}). Borrower requests and underwriter terms use the same intent structure: collateral, principal, rate, term, and execution path (the full quote specification is given in Section~\ref{sec:risk}). Each intent includes a nonce; the creation proof's on-chain execution writes a nullifier derived from the intent nonce, so a borrower can safely broadcast the same intent to multiple operators: only the first successful proof executes. The operator selects among criteria-compatible quotes; the creation proof verifies that signed intents are valid, criteria are satisfied, and proposed terms fall within the underwriter's committed policy. Routing discretion---which quotes to match and to which underwriter---is bounded by competition, not by the proof~\cite{daloc-decent2026}. Quote terms are visible only to the operator, the underwriter, and the borrower.

Participation requires no upfront capital lock. A borrower signs an off-chain intent and grants a token allowance to the hub, authorizing a future transfer but moving nothing. Capital is transferred only when a settlement proof executes the match on-chain; until then, funds remain in the participant's wallet.

\subsection{Criteria-Based Matching}

Beyond price compatibility, a match must satisfy \emph{criteria}: typed attributes attached to an intent (counterparty allowlists, jurisdictional restrictions, KYC-tier requirements, or sanctions-screen attestations). The operator treats a pair as compatible only if each side's criteria are satisfied by the other's attributes. Unrestricted participants cannot match against restricted entries whose criteria they do not satisfy; restricted participants match only against compatible counterparties. There is no separate permissioned market; entries in one order book vary only in how restrictive they are.

This avoids the fragmentation of permissioned forks: one liquidity surface, one risk engine, one settlement pipeline, one audit perimeter. Adding a new jurisdiction means adding a new attestation issuer, not deploying new contracts.

Criteria can be committed-only (stored as a hash, evaluated inside the proof, revealing only the boolean outcome). Counterparty attributes are typically committed-only, preserving privacy while still enforcing compatibility. The default is open: an unrestricted intent imposes no criteria on its counterparty. Criteria proofs are a protocol-level matching primitive; they do not constitute or replace compliance with applicable legal or regulatory obligations, which remain the responsibility of each participant.

\subsection{Markets}

Criteria restrict \emph{who} can match; markets define \emph{what} the matched position looks like. \daloc{} is organized around isolated lending markets. Each market is uniquely identified by a deterministic hash of its parameters: the collateral chain and asset, principal chain and asset, and four zero-knowledge program hashes that govern loan behavior, including accrual, liquidation, collateral policy, and oracle-dependent state transitions. All parameters are fixed at creation; a different accrual program, liquidation rule, collateral policy, or oracle policy constitutes a different market. Within a market's accrual program, rate model selection (fixed or variable) is a per-position decision set by the underwriter's committed policy (Section~\ref{sec:lp}).

Oracle selection follows the same immutability rule. In protocols such as Morpho Blue, the oracle is an explicit market parameter: each market points to a specific oracle contract, fixed at creation~\cite{morphoblue2023}. In \daloc{}, oracle logic is embedded in the market's zero-knowledge programs instead. A market program specifies which on-chain sources may be read, how prices or rates are transformed, and which freshness constraints must hold before a state transition is accepted. The protocol verifies the program output, not a protocol-wide oracle choice. Changing the oracle source, aggregation rule, freshness bound, or liquidation-price logic changes the program hash and therefore defines a new market.

Markets are created through the \emph{hub} contract (DalocHub), deployed at identical addresses on every supported chain (Section~\ref{sec:crosschain}). Creating a market from registered program hashes is permissionless; deploying a wholly new market \emph{type} (novel risk model with new proof programs) requires the program hashes to be registered through the hub's governance process. Each chain's hub instance is the sole token rail on that chain: all user-facing ERC-20 transfers route through it, creating a single per-chain authorization point for capital flows, intent cancellations, and global pause. The hub maintains zero persistent balance; protocol fees are deducted and routed to a fee-accumulator contract atomically at settlement time.

Different markets can encode different collateral strategies: a \emph{classical} market locks collateral as a liquidation hedge, while a more aggressive market can permit managed rehypothecation. The choice is a market-definition decision, not a protocol-level toggle.

Each market deploys issuer contracts on both the collateral and principal chains; each funded loan creates per-loan contracts at deterministic \texttt{CREATE2} addresses. Per-loan contracts are isolated (one per loan, not shared across borrowers), so one position's risk cannot cascade to another.

Isolation extends beyond individual positions to entire markets. Each market operates as an independent state machine: it maintains its own state and shares no global state with other markets. A market can access only its own balances; one market's bug or misconfiguration cannot reach into another's funds. Contract-level isolation is structural, but economic exposure is shared within an underwriter: bad debt from any position hits the common first-loss buffer, and if the buffer is exhausted, LP share price across the underwriter's entire portfolio is impaired (Section~\ref{sec:lp}). Multiple markets for the same collateral/principal pair may coexist under different risk parameters, oracle policies, rule sets, or execution operators; participants evaluate each market's operator, parameters, and track record before choosing where to transact.

\subsection{Hook Composition}

Lending operations are expressed as atomic hook chains attached to settlement events. Each transfer in the settlement tree carries optional \emph{pre-hooks} and \emph{post-hooks}: pre-hooks execute before the transfer (e.g.\ deploy a CDP), the transfer moves collateral or principal, and post-hooks execute after (e.g.\ supply to an external venue, borrow, bridge). All steps on a single chain must succeed or the entire on-chain operation reverts; cross-chain steps (bridge hooks) are committed atomically but execute asynchronously on the destination chain, stalling rather than reverting if the bridge is delayed (Section~\ref{sec:crosschain}).

Operators assemble hook chains and commit them into the settlement tree before on-chain execution. The settlement proof verifies that every hook is authorized by the market's ZK programs and that the resulting state satisfies the market's invariants. Borrowers sign high-level intents; operators compete to attach the cheapest valid execution plan. Intent cancellation is a direct user action requiring only the user's signature, with no proof and no operator: it takes effect immediately on-chain, and subsequent creation proofs verify cancellation status via storage proof. Margin adjustment (adding collateral) and direct repayment are likewise direct user actions~\cite{daloc-decent2026}.

\subsection{Programmable Credit}

Permissionless markets, hook composition, and constrained execution together form a programmable credit layer. A market creator writes ZK programs governing accrual, liquidation, and collateral policy; operators compete to assemble the cheapest valid execution plan from hooks; borrowers express only high-level intents.

Constrained execution environments extend this programmability to the destination chain. A margin agent on HyperEVM restricts borrowed capital to approved operations (perpetual trading only, no withdrawals), but the constraint boundary itself is programmable: HyperEVM contracts interact with HyperCore's perpetual engine via precompiles, enabling on-chain strategies within the lender-defined safety envelope:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Delta-neutral yield.} A borrower pledges stETH, borrows USDC, and the margin agent automatically opens a short ETH perpetual matching the collateral's notional. The position is market-neutral; net yield equals the staking rate minus funding cost minus the \daloc{} fixed borrowing rate. The constraint permits only a single, fully hedged position with no directional bets.
  \item \textbf{Automated stop-loss.} The margin agent monitors unrealized PnL via precompile reads and auto-closes the position if losses exceed a configurable threshold, bounding lender exposure during inactive periods.
  \item \textbf{Portfolio rebalancing.} The margin agent maintains multiple perpetual positions at target allocations, rebalancing when drift exceeds a bound and keeping margin utilization within the market's declared limits.
\end{itemize}
Because the lender's risk depends on what capital \emph{can} do, not on borrower intent, tighter constraints enable more favorable terms and may, in future, support under-collateralized credit lines.

\subsection{Operators and Underwriters}

An \emph{operator} matches borrower intents against underwriter capital and produces zero-knowledge proofs for each settlement action. Independent \emph{keepers} submit these proofs on-chain for execution fees; independent \emph{liquidators} enforce against positions that breach committed parameters for bonuses. The operator never holds tokens; its scope is matching and proof generation. Revenue is a matching fee on successful originations. Two behaviors remain unconstrained by proofs: liveness (whether to produce proofs at all) and ordering (which intents to match, and to which underwriter); both are bounded by competition and deadlines~\cite{daloc-decent2026}.

An \emph{underwriter} is an on-chain entity that pools LP capital, underwrites position risk, and stands as economic counterparty to borrowers (Section~\ref{sec:lp}). Each underwriter is scoped to one operator's registry, establishing clean separation: losses in one scope cannot reach another. Each underwriter commits a policy hash encoding its operating constraints: risk parameters, rate ranges, maturity limits, maximum exposure, bonus ranges, and sweep frequency. The operator routes intents to underwriters; the creation proof verifies that proposed terms fall within the underwriter's committed policy.

Multiple underwriter variants serve different strategies. An \emph{automated underwriter} originates positions if and only if they satisfy the committed policy hash: all decisions are deterministic and provable. A \emph{curator-managed underwriter} adds active management under an enforced mandate: a curator signs per-position authorizations within bounds checked at proof time~\cite{daloc-decent2026}. Other structures (direct lenders, external venue wrappers) plug into the same interface; the operator treats all variants uniformly.

Routing and capital sourcing are operator-level and underwriter-level concerns respectively, making the protocol extensible to new venues without core contract changes. \daloc{} provides the settlement rails; existing venues provide the liquidity.
