\section{Risk Quantification}
\label{sec:risk}

The underwriter's committed policy (Section~\ref{sec:lp}) sets the outer boundaries
(collateral classes, LTV ceilings, rate floors, concentration caps). The risk
engine sets the per-loan terms within those boundaries. This section specifies
how \daloc{} converts risk channels (Table~\ref{tab:risk}) into
loan-level loss distributions, underwriting terms, reserve requirements, and
buffer-adequacy checks before capital is deployed.

Each loan is treated as a collateralized credit instrument, not merely as an
exposure to collateral volatility. Structural credit models show that lender
risk depends on the relation between collateral value, debt, maturity, and
recovery, not on volatility alone~\cite{merton1974}. DeFi lending markets add
endogenous funding and liquidity risk: interest rates, utilization, and market
liquidity change with protocol conditions and can be correlated across venues
during stress~\cite{gudgeon2020}. Recent work models decentralized lending
contracts as option-like claims whose lender payoff depends on LTV, liquidation
rules, transaction costs, and hedging feasibility~\cite{szpruch2024}. Vault-based
credit adds another layer: depositors bear losses through liquidation failure,
oracle execution divergence, liquidity constraints, governance limits, and
smart-contract risk~\cite{zbandut2026}. For this reason, \daloc{} models the
full economic loss distribution after reserves, recovery, and buffer
commitments, rather than using volatility or liquidation probability as a
standalone proxy for lender risk.

\subsection{Risk Objective and Loss Definition}

The risk engine constrains economic loss to the lender, the underwriter, and
its first-loss buffer under both ordinary and stressed conditions.
A liquidation event is not itself a default. A position may be liquidated without
lender loss if the debt is repaid in full, while lender loss can arise when
liquidation is delayed, market depth is insufficient, execution costs are high,
or funding and settlement costs exhaust loan-specific reserves. Empirical work
on DeFi liquidations shows that liquidation incentives, collateral discounts,
and liquidator behavior materially affect realized outcomes~\cite{perez2021,qin2021}.
Recent work on liquidation dynamics further shows that transaction fees,
oracle-extractable value, and market microstructure can affect whether a
liquidation protects lenders or amplifies loss~\cite{sadeghi2026}. Underwriter-level
credit analysis therefore treats liquidation mechanics as only one component
of depositor loss, alongside oracle, liquidity, governance, and infrastructure
risks~\cite{zbandut2026}.

For each loan $i$, let $\rho_i$ denote a realized scenario path. A scenario path
includes collateral prices, funding rates, venue liquidity, oracle updates,
execution costs, bridge or settlement latency, and liquidation outcomes over
the loan horizon. Let $\mathrm{EAD}_i(\rho_i)$ be the exposure at close-out:
contractual debt, accrued obligations, funding deficits, execution costs, and
settlement costs under scenario $\rho_i$. Let $R^{\mathrm{pre}}_i(\rho_i)$ be
pre-buffer recovery: borrower repayments, collateral proceeds, and
loan-specific reserves available before any buffer payout. The gross
economic loss before buffer support is
\[
  L^{\mathrm{gross}}_i
  =
  \max\!\left(
    \mathrm{EAD}_i(\rho_i) - R^{\mathrm{pre}}_i(\rho_i),\; 0
  \right).
\]

If loan $i$ is covered by an underwriter with first-loss buffer commitment $A_i$,
the buffer payout and residual lender loss are
\[
  L^{\mathrm{buffer}}_i
  =
  \min\!\left(
    L^{\mathrm{gross}}_i,\; A_i
  \right),
\]
\[
  L^{\mathrm{net}}_i
  =
  \max\!\left(
    L^{\mathrm{gross}}_i - A_i,\; 0
  \right).
\]
Thus $L^{\mathrm{gross}}_i$ is the loss before buffer support,
$L^{\mathrm{buffer}}_i$ is the amount absorbed by the first-loss buffer, and
$L^{\mathrm{net}}_i$ is the residual loss borne by LPs through share-price
impairment. A loan can therefore be liquidated successfully without lender loss
when recovery covers the debt; the buffer absorbs shortfall up to its commitment;
residual loss, if any, falls to LPs.

The model separately reports three quantities. The liquidation probability is
the probability that the loan breaches its health factor and enters liquidation.
The loss probability is the probability that residual economic loss is positive,
$\Pr[L^{\mathrm{net}}_i > 0]$. Loss given default is the expected residual loss
conditional on a positive loss event,
\[
  \mathrm{LGD}_i
  =
  \mathbb{E}\!\left[
    L^{\mathrm{net}}_i
    \mid
    L^{\mathrm{net}}_i > 0
  \right].
\]

At portfolio level, losses are aggregated under the same joint scenario, preserving
dependence through common market, liquidity, oracle, funding, bridge, and
operational shocks. Expected shortfall is used as the primary tail-risk measure
because it captures the severity of losses beyond a quantile and supports
portfolio aggregation and constrained optimization~\cite{artzner1999,rockafellar2000}.
For a portfolio loss random variable $L$, the expected shortfall at confidence
level $\alpha$ is
\[
  \mathrm{ES}_{\alpha}(L)
  =
  \mathbb{E}\!\left[
    L \mid L \geq \mathrm{VaR}_{\alpha}(L)
  \right],
\]
with the confidence level, liquidation horizon, stress set, and admissibility
bounds disclosed by each underwriter and bounded by the underwriter's committed policy and declared precomputation kernel hash (Section~\ref{subsec:provable-risk-engine}).

\subsection{Dynamic Underwriting}

Every quote is produced from the current state of the collateral, principal asset, funding path, liquidation venues, and existing portfolio. The quote specifies at least
\begin{equation}
  q_i = \bigl(N_i,\, \lambda_i,\, \theta_i,\, r_i,\, T_i,\, B_i,\, \pi_i\bigr),
  \label{eq:quote}
\end{equation}
where $N_i$ (gross principal), $\lambda_i$ (origination LTV), and $\theta_i$ (liquidation threshold) define the position; $r_i$ (rate or committed rate-model identifier) and $T_i$ (term) define the economics; $B_i$ (reserve vector) sizes the hedging; and $\pi_i$ identifies the underwriter's committed policy hash.

Principal limits, rate, liquidation threshold, reserve sizing, and admissibility are outputs of the underwriting process, not protocol constants. Fixed contractual terms are not repriced ex~post; a deterioration in risk may trigger a top-up request, refinancing, or liquidation according to the market's pre-committed rules.

\subsection{Scenario Generation}

Risk is estimated under the real-world probability measure because the objective is capital adequacy, not only no-arbitrage valuation. The scenario generator reproduces volatility clustering, jumps, heavy tails, and the tendency of liquidity, funding rates, and operational latency to worsen together during stress~\cite{castro2021,chiu2023,gudgeon2020,heimbach2023,tovanich2023}. Challenger models (alternative specifications run against the primary model to detect divergence) run in parallel; parameter uncertainty is converted into conservative add-ons.

Each simulated path replays actual loan cash-flow logic (accrual, liquidation detection, and recovery), producing a distribution of economic loss, not merely of collateral prices. Recovery is modeled as endogenous to trade size, venue depth, and liquidator economics~\cite{perez2021,qin2021,sadeghi2026,tian2025}. For liquidity-forwarding loans, reserves are sized so that under modeled stress scenarios the protocol's liquidation completes before the venue's instant trigger fires; tail scenarios beyond the modeled set may still produce a gap (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\subsection{Provable Risk Engine}
\label{subsec:provable-risk-engine}

The risk engine separates computation into two layers: a precomputation kernel
and a serving path. The precomputation kernel is a hash-committed risk surface
that encodes the heavy simulation output used to
bound quote generation, reserve sizing, and portfolio aggregation. The serving
path answers individual quote requests against this committed kernel and
produces a zero-knowledge proof of correct computation.

\paragraph{Precomputation kernel.}
Simulation results are materialized into a multi-dimensional tensor indexed by
market-driven and loan-level parameters (volatility regime, LTV, rate, term).
Each cell stores break-even quantities, liquidation probabilities, loss quantiles,
reserve requirements, and admissibility bounds at which the protocol's expected
net loss is zero or better. Only the computationally heaviest quantities reside
in the kernel; all remaining quote parameters are derived in the serving
path. Recomputation happens periodically from fresh market data; between
updates, the same committed kernel serves all quotes for a given
collateral/principal pair.

\paragraph{Serving path.} When a borrower requests a quote, the serving layer maps the request into the kernel's parameter space and searches for the optimal $(\lambda_i,\theta_i,r_i,B_i)$ that satisfies the borrower's intent while remaining break-even or better for the lender. Interpolation over the discrete grid yields a continuous surface; optimization proceeds over it without Monte Carlo at serving time, reducing latency by orders of magnitude. Dedicated kernels serve rate-buffer and collateral-conversion-reserve sizing using the same procedure.

\paragraph{ZK provability.} The serving computation (kernel lookup, interpolation, and optimization) is deterministic given the kernel snapshot and borrower parameters. \daloc{} produces a zero-knowledge proof attesting that the quote was correctly derived from the declared kernel hash and risk policy. Settlement contracts verify this proof alongside the market's state-transition fact, ensuring that not only the executed loan terms but the underwriting logic that produced them is cryptographically auditable. Kernel precomputation, which involves stochastic Monte Carlo simulation, sits outside the proof boundary; what is proven is the deterministic path from a committed kernel to a specific quote.

\subsection{Funding and Execution Reserves}

For liquidity-forwarding loans, the engine sizes separate reserves for funding mismatch and execution cost. The rate buffer covers the expected shortfall of the maximum pathwise funding deficit over the loan term; the collateral conversion reserve covers stressed round-trip execution costs. Both are calibrated jointly so that a collateral crash, higher utilization, thinner liquidity, and longer settlement delay can occur in the same path~\cite{bastankhah2024b,baude2025,chitra2023,nadkarni2025}.

External-venue liquidity stress is treated as a first-class scenario: simulations
include high utilization, borrow-rate spikes, reserve pauses, delayed withdrawals,
chain downtime, bridge delay, and failed or partial unwinds. A route is rejected
when the required reserve, liquidation latency, or venue exposure exceeds the
market policy or underwriter policy.

\subsection{Pricing}

Each quote is priced from expected gross credit loss, the incremental tail capital consumed by the first-loss buffer, expected hedging costs, and operating costs~\cite{baude2025,halpern2024,szpruch2024}. Each underwriter optimizes its own objective within constraints set by the committed precomputation kernel and its published policy; quote competition selects the best compatible terms among feasible offers.

\subsection{Portfolio Aggregation and Concentration Risk}

Each new loan is assessed against the existing book, not on a standalone basis.
Portfolio aggregation is performed against the current precomputation kernel
(Section~\ref{subsec:provable-risk-engine}), which provides the committed risk
surface used for quote generation, reserve sizing, and admissibility checks.
Concentration limits cover counterparty, collateral asset, principal asset,
external venue, bridge path, oracle source, infrastructure dependency, and
market-operator dimensions.

The engine measures wrong-way risk: cases in which collateral falls while
funding rates, liquidation costs, venue utilization, bridge latency, or the
probability of venue failure rise. Diversification credit is recognized only when
offsets remain legally, technically, and operationally available through the
same close-out process~\cite{gordy2003,zbandut2025}. A portfolio that appears
diversified by asset ticker may still be concentrated in the same oracle, bridge,
liquidation venue, external lending protocol, or settlement operator; these
shared dependencies are treated as concentration factors rather than ignored
because the labels look different.

\subsection{Operational Risk and Model Governance}

Market and funding risks are estimated probabilistically where sufficient data exist. Sparse infrastructure failures (smart-contract, bridge, oracle, chain-halt, proof-system) are controlled through deterministic scenarios, exposure caps, and conservative add-ons rather than spuriously precise event frequencies. Each quote records the data snapshot, model hash, and calibration window used to produce it; recalibration is triggered when realized conditions deviate materially from model assumptions~\cite{bartoletti2025,zbandut2026}.
